A while ago, a friend asked me: "Where do I even start with learning AI?"

He sells coffee machines. He's not in tech, and he doesn't want to be. He wants one thing: **to stop losing his time to the same tasks every week.** Typing data into spreadsheets. Building forecasts from that data. Finding leads. Answering potential buyers who ask the same questions again and again.

If that sounds like you, this post is for you.

## Why most people never start

AI is huge. New models, new tools, and new "must-try" apps show up every week. So most people get stuck on two questions: "Where do I even start?" and "Am I already too late?"

My answer: **tech can wait. Your fundamentals can't.**

I'm not saying you shouldn't learn AI tools. But tools change every few months. What you learn about one tool today might be outdated next year. Fundamentals stay, and they're what make AI actually useful. For me, there are three:

1. **Domain knowledge.** You know your work better than any AI. My friend knows his customers, his products, and which numbers matter in his forecast. AI doesn't. Without this, you can't tell if AI's answer is good or wrong.
2. **Logic.** Breaking a big problem into small steps and finding the root cause. This is how you explain a task clearly to AI, and how you check its work.
3. **Creativity.** Seeing where AI could fit into your work, and trying another way when the first one doesn't work.

![The foundation is three solid blocks marked with a briefcase, a flowchart and a light bulb. On top, AI app windows are lifted away and replaced by a crane.](/images/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai/02-fundamentals.webp "The foundation stays. The tools on top keep changing.")

So no, you're not too late. If your fundamentals are strong, you can pick up any tool when you need it. And the first fundamental to use is logic: finding the real problem.

## Start with the problem, not the tool

Most people start by asking, "Which AI tool is the best?" I think that's backwards.

Start with the problem. If you know what the problem is, you're already halfway to the solution. And I don't mean the surface problem. I mean the **root cause**. Many problems share the same root cause. If you've fixed a problem but things still feel inefficient, that's usually a sign you haven't touched the root cause yet.

Here's an example I use a lot. Why were you late to the office?

- Why were you late? → I missed the bus.
- Why did you miss the bus? → I woke up late.
- Why did you wake up late? → My alarm didn't go off.
- Why didn't the alarm go off? → My phone died.
- Why did your phone die? → I fell asleep watching YouTube last night.

**That's the root cause.** "Set more alarms" won't fix it, because the phone will still die. The real fix is a sleep timer and a charging phone.

This technique is called the **5 Whys**. I won't explain all of it here, because [this guide from Adobe](https://business.adobe.com/blog/basics/5-whys-root-cause-analysis) does it better.

![A ball rolls down five steps, one for each why, and stops in the hole of the last step: the root cause.](/images/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai/03-five-whys.webp "Five whys down to the root cause.")

The good news: you don't have to do this alone. This is the first place where AI can help.

## Use AI to find the root cause

Asking "why" five times sounds easy. In practice, we stop too early. We stop at the first answer that sounds right, or we jump straight to a solution.

AI is good at keeping you honest here. It doesn't get tired of asking "why", and it doesn't let you skip ahead if you tell it not to. You bring the answers, because you know your work. AI brings the discipline.

Copy this prompt:

```text
I want to find the root cause of a problem at work:
[describe the task and why it bothers you].
Use the 5 Whys method.
Ask me one "why" question per message.
Don't suggest any solutions yet.
If my answer is vague, ask me to be more specific.
Stop when we reach a cause I can actually change,
then summarize the chain from the problem to the root cause.
```

Here's how it could go for my friend's spreadsheet problem. I don't know his exact answers, so this is just an illustration:

- Why do you type data into the spreadsheet by hand? → Orders come in from chats, calls, and notes.
- Why do they come from so many places? → Every customer orders in their own way.
- Why is there no single way to order? → There's no order form. I write down whatever they send.
- Why is there no order form? → Nobody made one. It has always been done like this.

Now the problem looks different. Typing isn't the real problem. It's a symptom. The root cause is that **orders don't arrive in one format**. If AI only helped him type faster, he would still be cleaning messy data every week.

## Then find the solution together

Only now, with the root cause in hand, do you start looking for a solution.

The biggest problem with AI here is that it doesn't know what's in your head. So it guesses, and it often guesses wrong. What finally made my results good was an **interview**: before AI suggests anything, make sure you both understand the problem the same way. Keep going through every confusing question and every open item until everything is clear.

Start a new chat with your root cause, and make AI ask *you*:

```text
I found the root cause of a problem at work: [your root cause].
Help me find a solution. Before you suggest anything, interview me.
Ask one question per message.
For each question, suggest the answer you recommend,
so I can just say "yes" or correct it.
Keep going until you understand the context, my constraints,
and the result I want.
Then summarize your understanding and give me two or three options.
```

![Two cubes pass questions and answers back and forth on a loop, and only then does the track lead into the working machine.](/images/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai/06-interview-loop.webp)

For complex problems, this can take a long time. But that's the process, and the answer is much better.

Fun fact: this post was written the same way. AI asked me questions one by one, I answered, and then it helped me put it together. The stories and opinions are still mine.

If you get comfortable with this, check out the [grilling skill by Matt Pocock](https://skills.sh/mattpocock/skills/grilling). It's built for stress-testing plans and ideas, and it's a good next step.

## AI is not a replacement

In both steps, you're the one with the answers. AI asks the questions and keeps the process honest, but **AI is not replacing you.** It's a tool to improve your speed, your quality, and the value of your work. You still understand your data. You still understand your customers. If AI's summary looks wrong, you're the one who has to say, "This is wrong."

## Start this week

If you only have time for one thing this week:

1. **Write down** every repetitive task you do for one week, and roughly how long each one takes.
2. **Pick the one** that takes the most time. Just one.
3. **Find its root cause** with AI, using the first prompt.
4. **Explore solutions** with AI, using the second prompt. Don't build anything yet.

Building the solution is a different story, and it's where most of my early AI work failed. That's the next post. For now, find the real problem first.
