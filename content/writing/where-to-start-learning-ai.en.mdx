A while ago, a friend asked me: "Where do I even start with learning AI?"

He sells coffee machines. He's not in tech, and he doesn't want to be. He wants one thing: **to stop losing his time to the same tasks every week.** Typing data into spreadsheets. Building forecasts from that data. Finding leads. Answering potential buyers who ask the same questions again and again.

If that sounds like you, this post is for you.

## Why most people never start

AI is huge. New models, new tools, and new "must-try" apps show up every week. So most people get stuck on two questions: "Where do I even start?" and "Am I already too late?"

My answer: **tech can wait. Your fundamentals can't.**

I'm not saying you shouldn't learn AI tools. But tools change every few months. What you learn about one tool today might be outdated next year. Fundamentals stay, and they're what make AI actually useful. For me, there are three:

1. **Domain knowledge.** You know your work better than any AI. My friend knows his customers, his products, and which numbers matter in his forecast. AI doesn't. Without this, you can't tell if AI's answer is good or wrong.
2. **Logic.** Breaking a big problem into small steps and finding the root cause. This is how you explain a task clearly to AI, and how you check its work.
3. **Creativity.** Seeing where AI could fit into your work, and trying another way when the first one doesn't work.

![A tower whose foundation is three fixed blocks for domain knowledge, logic and creativity, while the tool blocks on top keep being swapped out.](/images/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai/02-fundamentals.webp "The foundation stays. The tools on top keep changing.")

So no, you're not too late. If your fundamentals are strong, you can pick up any tool when you need it.

## Start with the problem, not the tool

Most people start by asking, "Which AI tool is the best?" I think that's backwards.

Start with the problem. This is where logic comes in. If you know what the problem is, you're already halfway to the solution. And I don't mean the surface problem. I mean the **root cause**. Many problems share the same root cause. If you've fixed a problem but things still feel inefficient, that's usually a sign you haven't touched the root cause yet.

Here's an example I use a lot. Why were you late to the office?

- Why were you late? → I missed the bus.
- Why did you miss the bus? → I woke up late.
- Why did you wake up late? → My alarm didn't go off.
- Why didn't the alarm go off? → My phone died.
- Why did your phone die? → I fell asleep watching YouTube last night.

**That's the root cause.** This is called the **5 Whys**: keep asking "why" until you reach the real reason.

![A ball rolls down five steps, one for each why, and stops in the hole of the last step: the root cause.](/images/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai/03-five-whys.webp "Five whys down to the root cause.")

Now look at the solutions. "Set more alarms" or "run faster to catch the bus" won't fix anything, because the phone will still die. The real fix is simple: set a sleep timer so videos stop on their own, and make sure your phone is charging before you sleep.

Do the same with your repetitive tasks at work.

AI is just a tool. A great tool is useless if you don't know what you need it for.

## My story: college notes

The first time I used AI to cut repetitive work was in college. I kept two sets of notes: handwritten notes in class, and detailed notes in Notion where I could see the history.

Moving everything from paper to Notion and cleaning it up was very repetitive. So I used AI to tidy up my notes into the format I had already built in Notion.

It worked really well. And I think the reason was how we split the work:

> **I wrote it. AI cleaned it up. I defined the structure.**

![Messy handwritten notes go into an AI machine on a conveyor belt and come out as a neat, structured stack.](/images/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai/04-who-does-what.webp)

Because of that, I still understood everything in my notes. AI didn't think for me. It only did the repetitive part.

## Honestly, it failed a lot at first

This is the part people rarely talk about.

In the beginning, I failed more than I succeeded. I didn't know the best structure yet, and AI back then wasn't as smart as it is now. I spent hours "debugging" the process until the result matched what I expected.

So if your first week feels slower, that's normal. **The time savings come later.** Once the process works, you save a lot of time, and you keep saving it every time you repeat the task.

![Two lines over time: manual work stays flat, while working with AI starts higher, then crosses below it.](/images/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai/05-time-curve.webp "Flat line: manual. Purple line: with AI. Slower first, faster later.")

## The key: get on the same page

The biggest problem with AI is that it doesn't know what's in your head. So it guesses, and it often guesses wrong.

What finally made my results good was an **interview**. Before AI does anything, make sure you both understand the task the same way. Keep going through every confusing question and every open item until everything is clear.

The easiest way is to make AI ask *you*. Copy this prompt:

```text
I want to [your task].
Before you start, interview me.
Ask one question per message.
For each question, suggest the answer you recommend,
so I can just say "yes" or correct it.
Keep going until you understand the context, the format,
and the result I want.
Then summarize your understanding before you start working.
```

![Two cubes pass questions and answers back and forth on a loop, and only then does the track lead into the working machine.](/images/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai/06-interview-loop.webp)

For complex tasks, this can take a long time. But that's the process, and the output is much better.

Fun fact: this post was written the same way. AI asked me questions one by one, I answered, and then it helped me put it together. The stories and opinions are still mine.

If you get comfortable with this, check out the [grilling skill by Matt Pocock](https://skills.sh/mattpocock/skills/grilling). It's built for stress-testing plans and ideas, and it's a good next step.

## Three levels, one step at a time

You don't need to build a fancy system right away. Think of it as three levels:

1. **Chat with AI.** Copy and paste into ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. Good for building or explaining forecasting formulas, or researching potential leads.
2. **AI inside the tools you already use.** For example, AI in Google Sheets, or turning a photo or PDF into a table. Good for data entry.
3. **Automation.** Workflows or chatbots that run on their own. Good for answering the same lead questions over and over.

Start at level 1. Move up when the current level feels easy.

## AI is not a replacement

If there's one thing I want you to remember: **AI is not replacing you.** It's a tool to improve your speed, your quality, and the value of your work. You still understand your data. You still understand your customers. If a forecast from AI looks strange, you're the one who has to say, "This is wrong."

## Start this week

If you only have time for one thing this week:

1. **Write down** every repetitive task you do for one week, and roughly how long each one takes.
2. **Ask "why"** about the one that takes the most time, just like the bus example. Why is it manual? Why are the formats different? Keep asking until you find the root cause.
3. **Pick one.** Just one.
4. **Let AI interview you** with the prompt above, and try it together.

It doesn't have to work on the first try. What matters is that you start.
