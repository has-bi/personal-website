In [the last post](/writing/where-to-start-learning-ai), we used AI to find the root cause of a problem. So now what?

Most people jump straight to "let's build an AI for it." I get it. Building is the fun part. But in my experience, a lot of problems don't need AI at all. Sometimes the fix is a better SOP. Sometimes it's a simple form. And sometimes you really do need to build something.

So how do you decide? I ask five questions, in this order. I'll walk through them with a real example: the membership program I built for [Abay Coffee and Listening Bar](https://abay-coffee.vercel.app/), a coffee shop I help run. Whenever you see an **At Abay** box, that's the example. Everything else applies to any problem.

## The problem

Abay had sales data from its **POS** (point of sale): the cashier app that records every order and payment. But that data was only per transaction. We didn't know **who** was buying, how much they usually spent, or when they came back. Without that, we couldn't plan promos or new menu items around real customers. That was the root cause.

There was also one condition we had to work with: the POS plan we subscribe to has **no API**. An API is the door that lets other apps read data directly. I know the tech folks are already thinking, "Why not just use the API?" There wasn't one. That condition shapes almost every decision below.

So the goal was clear: get to know our customers. The question was how.

## 1. Can you simplify it first?

Before thinking about tech, ask if there's a simpler way. A better SOP, a form, a habit. Sometimes that's all you need.

<Callout kind="At Abay">

We looked at two simple options:

- **A manual spreadsheet at the counter.** Baristas would need a proper device, every entry would look different, and typing data isn't their job. Their job is hospitality.
- **Stamp cards.** Most customers forget to bring the card. It also wastes paper, and printing isn't cheap.

</Callout>

Both options failed for the same reason: **the work landed on the wrong person.** So when you judge a "simple" solution, don't just ask "is this easy?" Ask **"easy for whom?"**

If no simple option works, the next question is whether the problem deserves something bigger.

## 2. Is it worth solving?

Look at four things:

- **Frequency.** How often does it happen?
- **Time per occurrence.** How long does it take each time?
- **Reach.** Who is affected?
- **Risk if it goes wrong.** What happens if your solution fails?

Then ask the opposite question too: **what's the risk if the manual process fails?** A small task can still be expensive if forgetting it hurts someone.

<Callout kind="At Abay">

- **Frequency:** every day. There are always customers.
- **Time per occurrence:** about two minutes a day, to export the customer data from the POS and replace it in the spreadsheet by hand.
- **Reach:** the owner and the customers.
- **Risk if the solution fails:** one transaction isn't recorded, and it can be fixed in the POS.
- **Risk if the manual process fails:** the daily update can never be forgotten. If it is, customers can't redeem their points at the counter.

</Callout>

These four things also tell you what success looks like, so write it down before you build. Start with a baseline, like the list of tasks and times from the first post. Then decide what you'll measure.

<Callout kind="At Abay">

What we decided to measure:

- How many customers are registered.
- How many repeat transactions are linked to a customer.
- How much time the daily update takes.

</Callout>

And set your expectations. Every change meets resistance at first. That's normal, and it doesn't mean your solution failed. Decide how long people should need to get used to it, and what success rate is good enough.

If it's worth solving, the next step is to describe the solution.

## 3. What's the solution, done by hand?

Forget tools for a moment. If one person did this by hand every day, what would they do? Write the steps down. There's no tech decision in them yet. That's the point: now you know exactly what any tool would need to do.

<Callout kind="At Abay">

1. Export the customer data from the POS.
2. Put it into a spreadsheet that acts as the customer database.
3. Let customers check their points and baristas redeem them, based on that spreadsheet.

</Callout>

## 4. Build or buy?

Now check if a tool already does this. If the problem is personal or specific to you, I lean toward building. You learn a lot along the way, and that learning compounds. If the problem sits at team or company level, build vs buy needs a much more careful decision.

Even for a personal problem, spend ten minutes checking if a tool already solves it. Build because you chose to, not because you didn't look.

<Callout kind="At Abay">

- The solution needs data from the POS, and the POS has no API on our plan.
- Loyalty apps couldn't connect to it.
- Upgrading the POS just for a loyalty program didn't make sense financially.

So we built something around what already existed.

</Callout>

## 5. What form should it take?

Look at the manual steps again. If someone has to do them over and over, that's the moment a solution changes form. Not before.

To pick the form, ask two questions about the steps:

- **Does it repeat?** Or does it only come up now and then?
- **Does it need judgement?** A clear rule means the same input always leads to the same action. Judgement means someone has to read, interpret, or decide.

![A two by two grid. Rare and clear rules: an SOP or checklist. Rare and needs judgement: AI as a chat partner. Repeats and clear rules: automation, highlighted for Abay. Repeats and needs judgement: AI plus automation. Below: if someone needs to see or act on the result, add an interface.](/images/writing/do-you-even-need-ai/matrix.webp)

Then one more question: **does someone need to see the result or act on it?** If yes, the solution also needs an interface, like a dashboard, a page, or a notification.

<Callout kind="At Abay">

- Every step repeats daily and follows a clear rule. So the form is **automation, not AI**.
- Two groups of people act on the data. So there are **two interfaces**: a member page where customers check their points, and a staff page where baristas redeem them.

</Callout>

I didn't see that right away. My very first question to AI was: "Can an AI agent log in to the POS and download the report for me every day?" The answer surprised me. Yes, it could, but it shouldn't. An AI agent can give a slightly different result each run, and it can click the wrong thing. A script does exactly the same steps every time. So the advice was: **use AI to write the script, not to run the task every day.**

![A chat where I ask AI, in Indonesian, how to automatically download a report from a POS without an API and upload it to Google Drive. The answer recommends a Playwright script on a schedule, with AI used to write the script.](/images/writing/do-you-even-need-ai/pos-initial-chat.webp "My first question to AI, in Indonesian. The short answer: it can, but use a script instead.")

Put together, the final setup looks like this. A browser automation (Playwright) logs in to the POS, exports the customer data, and uploads it to Google Drive. Every morning before the shop opens, Google Apps Script updates the customer spreadsheet. Both pages read from that spreadsheet.

![Every morning: the POS data is exported by Playwright, saved to Google Drive, and loaded by Apps Script into a customer spreadsheet. Customers check their points on the website and baristas redeem them on a staff page.](/images/writing/do-you-even-need-ai/system-flow.webp)

## After launch

The resistance I expected in step 2 showed up quickly.

<Callout kind="At Abay">

- **Baristas didn't know how to redeem points.** So I made the staff page do just two things: check a customer's points, and redeem an available promo. A redeemed promo can't be used again.
- **Some customers didn't want to share their phone number.** So they can now sign up with email.

</Callout>

Neither problem meant the solution failed. Both were signs that something was still too hard for someone. Each time, the answer was to make it simpler.

After one month, here are the numbers we said we would measure:

<Callout kind="At Abay · one month">

- **Every** repeat transaction is linked to a customer.
- The daily update takes **0 seconds** instead of 2 minutes.
- **50+** customers are registered. Before, there were zero.

</Callout>

The time saved is the smallest win here. The bigger ones: nobody has to remember a daily task anymore, and the shop now has customer data that simply didn't exist before. **Some solutions save time. The best ones make something possible that wasn't possible before.**

![Three wins, from smallest to biggest: two minutes a day saved, no daily task to remember, and zero to more than 50 registered customers in one month.](/images/writing/do-you-even-need-ai/value.webp)

## So where does AI fit?

Look back at the final setup: there's no AI inside it. But I used AI at every step of getting there. It helped me brainstorm the problem, explore solutions, build quick prototypes, and write the final code.

That's the shift I keep seeing. **AI doesn't have to be inside your solution. It can be what helps you build it.** The barrier to building your own tools is much lower than it used to be, so solving your own problems is now a real option for one person, not just for a team of engineers.

## Your checklist

Before you build anything, ask:

1. Can I simplify it first? Easy for whom?
2. Is it worth solving? What's my baseline, and what will I measure?
3. What's the solution if I do it by hand?
4. Build or buy?
5. What form should it take? An SOP, AI, automation, or AI + automation? Does someone need an interface?
